At a bustling coffee shop in downtown San Francisco, two computer science professors meet to discuss the future of artificial intelligence.

Dr. Chen: Good to see you, Dr. Williams. How's your semester going?
Dr. Williams: It's been incredible. My machine learning class has 500 students this year. Five years ago, it was 50.
Dr. Chen: Same here. The demand for AI education is exploding. But I worry we're not teaching the right things.
Dr. Williams: What do you mean?
Dr. Chen: We focus so much on technical skills that we forget about ethics and societal impact. Our students can build neural networks, but can they evaluate whether they should?
Dr. Williams: That's a fair point. I've started including ethics discussions in my lectures. Last week, we debated facial recognition technology.
Dr. Chen: How did that go?
Dr. Williams: It was eye-opening. Half the students supported it for security, half opposed it for privacy. But they all agreed it needed regulation.
Dr. Chen: Good. That's progress. What about bias in AI systems?
Dr. Williams: I show them examples. Like how some hiring algorithms discriminate against women, or how facial recognition has higher error rates for people of color.
Dr. Chen: Those examples are powerful. Do you think we need a separate ethics course?
Dr. Williams: Absolutely. And it should be mandatory. Every computer science graduate should understand the societal implications of their work.
Dr. Chen: I agree. What about the job market? Are our students prepared?
Dr. Williams: The technical ones are. But employers are increasingly looking for soft skills too. Communication, teamwork, critical thinking.
Dr. Chen: We should incorporate more group projects and presentations.
Dr. Williams: Exactly. And more interdisciplinary work. AI touches every field now - healthcare, finance, education, art.
Dr. Chen: Speaking of art, what do you think about AI-generated content?
Dr. Williams: It's fascinating and terrifying. The technology is amazing, but the implications for copyright, authenticity, and employment are huge.
Dr. Chen: Students ask me if AI will replace programmers.
Dr. Williams: What do you tell them?
Dr. Chen: I say AI will change programming, not replace it. The programmers who can work with AI will thrive. Those who can't will struggle.
Dr. Williams: Same with teaching. AI can help with grading and tutoring, but the human connection in education is irreplaceable.
Dr. Chen: Well said. Shall we get another coffee?
Dr. Williams: Please. And let's continue this conversation. We need more of these discussions in academia.
Dr. Chen: Agreed. The future of AI depends on how we educate the next generation.
